# Grounded theory

Read this when the question is about process and explanation rather than pattern description: how adoption happens, how trust forms, what accounts for variation between cases.

## Check feasibility before agreeing to it

Grounded theory requires iterative data collection, where analysis of early data determines who and what you sample next. If the corpus is closed (a set of transcripts already recorded, no further access to participants), full grounded theory is not available. Say so before starting rather than producing something labeled grounded theory that skipped its defining feature.

With a closed corpus, honest options:

- **Reflexive TA with a process orientation.** Themes organized around stages or mechanisms, without theoretical claims.
- **Grounded-theory-informed analysis.** Constant comparison and memoing applied to existing data, labeled as informed by grounded theory rather than as grounded theory.

Both are respectable. Neither should claim theoretical saturation.

## Variants

| Variant | Key figures | Stance |
|---|---|---|
| Classic | Glaser | Theory emerges; researcher stays neutral; no literature review upfront |
| Straussian | Strauss and Corbin | Structured coding paradigm (conditions, actions, consequences) |
| Constructivist | Charmaz | Theory is co-constructed; researcher position is part of the analysis |

Constructivist grounded theory is the usual default in applied health and program research, and it sits most comfortably alongside the reflexivity practices in this skill.

## Procedure

**1. Initial coding.** Line-by-line, using gerunds where possible ("waiting for access," "working around formats" rather than "access" or "formats"). Gerunds keep the analysis on action and process, which is what stops grounded theory collapsing into a topic list.

**2. Constant comparison.** Compare incident to incident, then code to code, then category to category, continuously. Every new piece of data is asked whether it fits, extends, or challenges what is already built.

**3. Memo throughout.** Memos are where the theory is actually written. Log them dated, with the codes they discuss and the turn IDs behind them. A grounded theory without memos has no development history and cannot be defended.

**4. Focused coding.** Promote the most analytically useful initial codes and use them to sort large volumes of data. Discard codes that do not do work.

**5. Theoretical sampling.** Collect more data aimed at developing weak categories, if collection is open. Record the decisions: which gap prompted which sampling choice.

**6. Theoretical saturation.** Legitimate here, and only here among the methods in this skill. Saturation means new data stops adding properties or dimensions to categories, not that you stopped hearing new topics and not that you ran out of budget. Defend it with the specific categories that stabilized, or do not claim it.

**7. Theoretical integration.** Build the categories into an explanatory account: a core category or process, with conditions, mechanisms, and consequences. State the scope conditions, meaning where the account holds and where it should not be applied.

## Traps

- **Grounded theory as a label for inductive coding.** Without constant comparison, memos, and a theoretical account, it is thematic analysis with different branding.
- **Saturation as a stopping excuse.** Claiming it because collection ended is the single most common misuse in published work.
- **Topic categories instead of process categories.** "Access" is a topic. "Rationing effort while waiting for access" is a process.
- **Theory that restates the data.** If the model is a list of what participants said, no integration happened.
- **Skipping scope conditions.** A theory with no stated limits invites application where it does not hold.
