## 1. Which TA are you doing

Braun and Clarke distinguish three families. Mixing their vocabulary is the fastest way to signal that the analysis was not thought through.

| Variant | Coding logic | Reliability stance | Themes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Coding reliability TA** | Codebook fixed early, applied by multiple coders | Inter-rater reliability, kappa, consensus meetings | Often predetermined domains |
| **Codebook TA** (framework, template analysis) | Structured codebook, allows some inductive additions | Some coder comparison, less statistical | Partly predetermined |
| **Reflexive TA** | Coding is organic and evolves; single analyst common | Rigor comes from depth of engagement and transparency, not agreement metrics | Constructed by the analyst, always analytic outputs |

This skill does reflexive TA. Consequences worth internalizing:

- Analyst subjectivity is a resource, not contamination to be controlled.
- Codes may be renamed, merged, or dropped at any point.
- Two competent analysts can produce different valid theme sets from the same corpus. That is not a defect.
- There is no "correct" theme set to be recovered, so agreement statistics measure the wrong thing.

If a stakeholder needs codebook TA or coding reliability TA (common in regulated settings or multi-site studies), say so and switch method explicitly. Do not bolt kappa onto reflexive TA.

## 2. The six phases in detail

Phases are recursive, not sequential. Moving backwards is normal and a sign the analysis is working.

**1. Familiarization.** Read the full corpus before coding. Listen to audio if available, since tone carries hedging and sarcasm that transcripts flatten. Write casual notes. Resist coding here; the goal is a feel for the whole.

**2. Generating initial codes.** Work through the corpus systematically, labeling anything relevant to the research interest. Codes are analytic units, small and specific. Two orientations, usually mixed:
- *Data-driven*: label what is there.
- *Research-question-driven*: label what speaks to what you were asked.

**3. Generating initial themes.** Cluster codes by shared meaning. Ask what pattern is present across cases, not what subject areas exist. Candidate themes at this stage are provisional. Some codes become themes, some collapse, some are set aside.

**4. Developing and reviewing themes.** Test candidates against coded extracts, then the whole corpus. Themes are asked to justify their existence. Typical actions: merge two thin themes with the same underlying concept, split a theme whose extracts pull apart, promote a subtheme, discard a theme built on the analyst's expectation.

**5. Refining, defining, naming.** Write the definition first, then name from the definition. If the definition is hard to write, the theme is not yet a theme.

**6. Writing up.** Interweave analytic narrative with data extracts. Extracts illustrate the claim; they do not replace it. A results section that is mostly quotes with connective tissue has not done the analytic work.

## 3. Themes vs topic summaries

The single most common critique of published TA is that reported "themes" are domain summaries: everything participants said about subject X, gathered under the heading X.

Diagnostic questions:

- Does the theme name contain a claim, or only a subject? "Trust in automated outputs" is a subject. "Trust survives errors when the tool shows its reasoning" is a claim.
- Could you state the central organizing concept in one sentence without listing the contents?
- Would removing the theme name leave a reader able to guess it from the extracts? If the extracts only share a topic, the theme is a summary.
- Are subthemes just the same topic sliced into narrower topics? That is a summary with extra structure.

Repair options: find the shared meaning and rename; split into two themes with distinct concepts; dissolve back into codes and recluster; or keep it as a descriptive appendix section clearly labeled as context rather than a theme.

## 4. Reflexivity in practice

Keep a running `reflexivity.md`. It is not decoration; it is where the rigor of a single-analyst method actually lives.

Log:

- **Starting assumptions.** What you expected to find before reading, including expectations inherited from the person who commissioned the analysis.
- **Stakes.** Who benefits from which finding. In applied program settings the pull is usually toward confirming a known problem or protecting a product.
- **Decisions and reversals.** Themes killed, merged, renamed, and why. This log is what you show when asked how the theme set was built.
- **Surprises.** Data that did not fit. These are load-bearing.
- **Reads you chose not to make.** Latent interpretations you considered and rejected as unsupported.

For AI-assisted analysis, add an honesty note: the analyst does not have embodied presence in the room, tone and body language are lost, and familiarization happens in one pass rather than over weeks. Those are real limitations on the depth of latent interpretation available. State them in the method note rather than implying full immersion.

## 5. Quality criteria and what reviewers ask

Braun and Clarke's own standards call for themes with central organizing concepts, analysis that goes beyond data summary, transparency about process, and coherence between stated epistemology and what was actually done.

Questions to be ready for:

- *How many coders?* One is methodologically appropriate for reflexive TA. Rigor comes from depth and transparency. Do not apologize for it.
- *What was your inter-rater reliability?* Not applicable, and explain why: there is no single correct coding to agree on.
- *Did you reach saturation?* Not a reflexive TA concept. Meaning is constructed, not exhausted. Speak instead to corpus adequacy for the claims made.
- *How do we know this is not just your interpretation?* It is an interpretation, disciplined by systematic coding of the full corpus, an audit trail from claim to turn ID, and a documented reflexivity log.
- *Is this generalizable?* No, and it was not meant to be. It is transferable insight about a specific group in a specific context, with the context described well enough for readers to judge fit.

## 6. Concepts that do not belong

- Inter-rater reliability, kappa, percentage agreement
- Data saturation
- Code frequency as evidence of importance
- "Themes emerged" (they were constructed; passive discovery language misrepresents the method)
- Member checking as validation of correctness (it can be a data source, not a truth test)
- Sample size calculations

## 7. Related methods

Reflexive TA is one option among several, and picking it by default is how applied work ends up with a method that does not answer the question. The selection table in SKILL.md covers the full set. The neighbours most often confused with reflexive TA:

- **Framework analysis** (`framework-analysis.md`) when cross-case comparison or multi-analyst work matters. Closest sibling, different rigor logic.
- **Content analysis** (`content-analysis.md`) when prevalence is the actual question. Counted codes are not themes.
- **Grounded theory** (`grounded-theory.md`) when a process account is needed and data collection is still open.
- **IPA** (`ipa.md`) when lived experience in a small homogeneous sample is the object.
- **Discourse or narrative analysis** (`discourse-narrative.md`) when how something was said is the object.
- **Rapid qualitative analysis** (`rapid-qual.md`) when the deadline makes six phases impossible. Label it rapid analysis, not reflexive TA, and state what was skipped.

Recommending the better-fitting method when TA is the wrong tool is more useful than delivering a weak TA.
