# Interpretative phenomenological analysis (IPA)

Read this when the object of interest is how specific people make sense of a significant experience: living with a diagnosis, caring for a relative, going through a trial, adapting a working life around a condition.

## When IPA fits, and when it does not

IPA needs:

- **Small, purposively homogeneous samples.** Typically 3 to 10 participants who share the experience. Large samples defeat the idiographic commitment.
- **Rich, first-person data.** In-depth semi-structured interviews, usually one participant at a time.
- **Experience as the object.** Not opinions about a product, not feedback on a process.

IPA does not fit workshop transcripts, group demos, or multi-speaker feedback sessions. Group data flattens the individual account that IPA is built to preserve. If a user asks for IPA on that kind of corpus, say why it will not work and offer reflexive TA instead. This is a common request and worth handling clearly rather than complying.

## Procedure

IPA is idiographic first: each case is analyzed completely before moving to the next, and cross-case work happens only at the end.

**1. Read and reread one transcript.** Stay with a single participant. Bracket what earlier cases suggested.

**2. Exploratory noting.** Three kinds of note, kept distinguishable:
- *Descriptive*: what the participant is talking about
- *Linguistic*: how they say it, including hesitation, metaphor, repetition, pronoun shifts
- *Conceptual*: interrogative notes about what the account means, held as questions rather than conclusions

**3. Experiential statements.** Turn the notes into concise statements that keep the participant's voice while carrying analytic weight. These stay close to the person, not the group.

**4. Connect and cluster within the case.** Group statements into personal experiential themes for that participant, with a structure that reflects their account rather than a template.

**5. Repeat fully for the next case**, then the next. Resist importing the previous case's structure.

**6. Cross-case analysis.** Build group experiential themes across participants only after every case is complete. Report which participants contribute to each, and be explicit where a theme rests on a minority. Convergence and divergence are both findings.

## The double hermeneutic

The analyst is making sense of the participant making sense of their experience. Two consequences worth carrying into the write-up:

- Interpretation goes beyond description, but stays anchored to what the participant said. Every interpretive claim is followed by the extract that grounds it.
- Analyst positioning belongs in the account. IPA does not pretend to a view from nowhere.

```markdown
- "[verbatim]" — P05 (T208)

## Case-level structures
[Per participant: their personal experiential themes, briefly, so the idiographic layer stays visible]

## Reflexive account
[How the analyst's position shaped the reading]
```

## Traps

- **Sample too large or too varied.** Depth becomes impossible and the result is a weak TA with IPA vocabulary.
- **Cross-case work started early.** The most common failure. Once the first case's structure is imported, the later participants stop being heard.
- **Extracts trimmed to soundbites.** IPA claims need enough of the participant's speech to let a reader judge the interpretation.
- **Interpretation without anchoring.** Conceptual notes are questions until an extract supports them.
- **Prevalence framing.** "6 of 8 participants" language belongs to other methods. Report convergence and divergence in prose.
