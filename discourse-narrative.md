# Discourse and narrative analysis

Read this when *how* something is said is the object of interest rather than what was said: how a problem gets framed, whose account is treated as authoritative, how blame and credit are distributed, how people position themselves and each other.

Both approaches treat language as doing work rather than reporting facts. That distinguishes them from every other method in this skill, where language is treated as a window onto content.

## Which one

| Approach | Object | Typical question |
|---|---|---|
| **Discourse analysis** | Patterns of language use and their effects | How is the problem constructed, and what does that construction make possible or unthinkable? |
| **Critical discourse analysis** | Language and power | Whose interests does this framing serve? What is naturalized as inevitable? |
| **Narrative analysis** | Story structure and identity work | What story is being told, with whom as protagonist, and toward what ending? |

## Discourse analysis procedure

**1. Choose the discursive object.** Name what construction you are examining ("the AI tool as collaborator versus as reviewer," "data sharing as duty versus as risk"). Without this the analysis wanders.

**2. Locate every instance** where the object appears, with turn IDs. Include instances where it appears by absence.

**3. Identify interpretative repertoires.** The recurring ways of talking about the object: shared vocabulary, stock phrases, metaphors, familiar arguments. Name each and show its markers.

**4. Analyze function and effect.** For each repertoire, what does it accomplish in the moment? Common functions: justifying inaction, distributing responsibility, managing accountability, establishing credentials, pre-empting criticism.

**5. Attend to variation.** The same speaker using incompatible repertoires at different moments is a finding, not an inconsistency to resolve. It shows the repertoires are resources being deployed rather than beliefs being reported.

**6. Note what the framing closes off.** The most useful discourse finding is often the option that becomes unsayable once a particular construction is in play.

## Narrative analysis procedure

**1. Identify narrative units.** Stretches with beginning, middle, and end, or with an evaluative point. Not every stretch of talk is a narrative.

**2. Analyze structure.** Orientation (setting, characters), complication, evaluation (why this story is worth telling), resolution, coda. The evaluation clause is where the teller's stance sits, and it is the part most often skipped.

**3. Analyze positioning.** Who is protagonist, who is antagonist, who has agency, who is acted upon. Where the teller places themselves relative to institutions matters in applied program work.

**4. Analyze the arc.** Progressive (things improving), regressive (declining), stable. Track whether the arc shifts mid-account.

**5. Compare across tellers.** Shared plot structures, canonical stories the group draws on, counter-narratives that resist them.

```markdown
## Method note

## [Repertoire or narrative type 1]: [name]

## Variation and contradiction
[Same-speaker shifts, competing framings, contested ground]

## What this means for [the practical question]
[Implications, stated as implications rather than as participant preferences]
```

## Traps

- **Sliding into content analysis.** Once the write-up reports what people think about a topic, the method has been abandoned.
- **Extracts too short.** Discursive moves need surrounding turns to be visible. A clipped quote cannot show a function.
- **Treating repertoires as personality.** They are resources speakers use, not stable attitudes. A speaker using two is normal.
- **Critique without evidence.** Critical discourse analysis is not a license to assert interests. The claim still has to be shown in the language.
- **Overclaiming reach.** A framing observed in one meeting is not an institutional discourse. Name the level you can support.
